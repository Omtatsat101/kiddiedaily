Science news, great photos, sky alerts.

Astronomer Alan Stern – who was principal investigator on the New Horizons mission to Pluto – claims that “most planetary astronomers” want Pluto to be a planet again. He’s the leader of what we might call Team Planet-Pluto. Astronomer Michael Brown – his principal adversary – claims that “Pluto just isn’t a planet.” Hear their contrary views in this 2026 video, hosted by EarthSky’s Deborah Byrd.

Watch in the player above, or on YouTube. On August 24, 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) announced it had re-classified Pluto as a dwarf planet. Moreover, it declared that Pluto was “not a planet.” From 1930 until that day, Pluto had been considered a planet, and the outermost world of the solar system.

As technology advanced, though, astronomers began to see fainter and fainter objects in our neighborhood. That led them to recognize Pluto as only the largest of many small bodies in the outer solar system. So is Pluto a planet or not?

Today, Pluto is known as a dwarf planet, and Neptune – 8th world from the sun – is considered the outermost of our solar system’s classical planets. It all came about because the IAU formulated and pushed through a new definition of what it means to be a planet. But astronomers, and a small segment of the public, are still angry about it.

And what would happen if Pluto were re-classified as a planet today? The video below, produced by EarthSky’s Deborah Byrd, has part of the answer. Prior to 2006, astronomers hadn’t gotten around to establishing clear standards for categorizing a solar system “planet” versus a “dwarf planet.” They began to see a need when many small bodies – such as Haumea and Makemake – began to be discovered in the outer solar system.

Eris, also considered a dwarf planet, has even more mass than Pluto (though it’s not quite as large). So if Pluto is a planet, why shouldn’t Eris be granted planet status as well? That was the question the IAU asked itself, which led to its formation of a Planet Definition Committee and ultimately the 2006 decision.

Science news, great photos, sky alerts. On August 23, 1966, Lunar Orbiter 1 took the 1st-ever photo of the Earth from the moon. It shows half of Earth, shot with 1960s technology, seen from the moon’s distance of 236,000 miles (380,000 km).
